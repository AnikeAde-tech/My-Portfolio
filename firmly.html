01 — the context

Software for people who are personally liable

Firmly is practice management for Nigerian law firms: intake, conflict checks, matters, documents, billing, and the compliance obligations sitting under all of it. Clio, rebuilt for a legal system with its own court hierarchy, its own regulator, and its own rules about what a lawyer must be able to prove after the fact.

Most firms here still run on WhatsApp, Excel and paper. Asking them to move isn't a productivity pitch. A lawyer who misses a filing deadline or takes a client they're conflicted on doesn't have a bad quarter. They answer to the Nigerian Bar Association. Every decision had to survive one question: what happens to me if this is wrong?

02 — the problem

Regulation isn't a module you add

The requirements arrived as a compliance list. Conflict checks under Rule 17 of the Rules of Professional Conduct. Practising certificate validity. NDPR data handling. Anti-money-laundering thresholds. Six-year retention. The obvious move is to build the product, then add a compliance section.

That fails for a specific reason. Compliance a lawyer can navigate around is compliance they will navigate around at 11pm before a filing, and the firm carries the consequence, not the software.

The rule isn't satisfied by a lawyer following it. It's satisfied by the firm being able to prove they did.

Underneath that sat a second problem of pure scale: eight roles, each with its own scope of visible records. Every screen in the product has eight correct versions, and only one of them is the one you happen to be designing.

03 — what I decided

Four calls that shaped the platform

decision 01

The conflict check blocks. It doesn't warn.

When a new client cross-references against an existing client, opposing party or witness, the matter goes to a terminal Rejected–Conflict state set by the system, with an audit entry no role can delete: not the partner, not the firm admin.

A warning banner would have been kinder and would have been ignored. The audit entry also does double duty: Rule 17 requires a written record of conflict checks, and this is that record, produced without anyone having to remember to write it.

what it costThe product tells firms they cannot take paying work, with no override. That's a hard thing to sell and it was the most argued decision in the spec. A conflict check with an override is a conflict check that didn't happen.
decision 02

An expired certificate restricts. It doesn't lock out.

Nigerian lawyers need a valid Annual Practising Certificate, and expiry has to change what someone can do. The naive build locks them out, which turns a paperwork lapse into a client emergency, mid-matter, possibly mid-hearing.

I designed it as a countdown and a downgrade rather than a cliff. Alerts at 60, 30 and 7 days. Then read-only rather than a closed door: they keep their matters, their calendar, their documents. They just can't act.

what it costA Restricted state that had to be designed and specified across every module: a lot of screens for a condition most users will never hit, and the state that took longest to get right in the system.
decision 03

Design for the adjournment, not the timeline

Most case management software assumes a matter progresses. Nigerian practice doesn't work that way: a case goes Active, gets adjourned, gets a new date, goes Active again, and can loop like that for years.

Treating that as an exception buries the only information the lawyer actually needs, which is the next date, and what happened at the last one. I made the loop the primary structure: adjournment history as a first-class, immutable record on the matter, and the next hearing date as the most prominent thing on the screen.

what it costThe matter view is dominated by dates rather than substance, which looked wrong to stakeholders reviewing it against international legal software. It's right for the market it's built for.
decision 04

Eight approval gates, one inbox

Case acceptance, KYC, engagement letters, invoices, court filings, user activation, case closure, disbursements. Each needs a partner or admin to sign off. Left in place, that's eight surfaces a partner has to remember to check, and approvals become the bottleneck the whole firm complains about.

Everything pending routes to a single inbox with inline review, so a partner clears the queue in one place instead of hunting for it. Rejection always requires a written reason, because a rejection without one just restarts the work.

what it costPulling approvals out of their context means each inbox item has to carry enough of that context to decide on. That's a much heavier component than a list row, and a real engineering cost.

04 — what shipped

Live, in beta, with real firms

The platform is live and onboarding partner firms. Adoption numbers belong to the client and aren't mine to publish, so here's the scope of what I designed.

  • 8User roles, on a two-axis model where role sets what you can do and scope sets which records you can do it to.
  • 8Approval gates, each producing an immutable audit entry with approver identity, timestamp and reason.
  • 14Nigerian practice areas mapped to their courts and governing statutes.
  • 7Matter states in a controlled machine, with defined transitions and a defined owner for each.
05 — in hindsight

What I'd do differently

I specified the full permission model before testing a screen with a real associate. It's coherent on paper and I still think the two-axis model is right, but the roles firms actually use don't map cleanly onto our eight. Small practices have one person doing four of them. That conversation belonged in week two, not in beta feedback.

I also under-designed the beginning. The spec is thorough about what a busy firm sees and thin about day one, when a firm has finished onboarding and faces a dashboard of empty widgets. That's the screen deciding whether they come back tomorrow, and it got the least attention.
