I designed a cross-border payment app for African freelancers around the only number that matters: what actually lands in your account.

01 — the context

Paid in dollars, living in naira

A designer in Lagos invoices a client in Berlin for $500. Three things then happen that nobody designed on purpose: the money takes a week, the exchange rate moves while it travels, and a series of fees come off it in places the freelancer can't see. What arrives is a naira figure that has no obvious relationship to the number on the invoice.

PayPro exists for that gap: multi-currency payments, invoicing, escrow and withdrawals for African freelancers working with clients abroad. Two sides, two different fears. The freelancer has done work and not been paid before. The client is sending money across a border to someone they've never met, in a market they've been warned about.

02 — the problem

Thirteen features and one actual job

The brief listed thirteen: multi-currency, instant transfers, invoicing, payment requests, escrow, recurring billing, expense tracking, reporting, fraud protection, late payment protection, scheduling, referrals, withdrawals. All reasonable. Built with equal weight, three products.

Underneath all of it sat one job. A freelancer opens this app to answer a single question, and it is never “how are my expenses categorised.”

Am I going to get paid, and how much of it is actually mine?

Everything else on the roadmap had to earn its place by serving that question or get out of its way. In four weeks, that ordering was the design work, not the screens.

03 — what I decided

Four calls about money you can't fully promise

decision 01

Show the number that will land, not the number on the invoice

The flattering design shows $500 in large type. The honest one shows what hits the freelancer's bank account after conversion and fees, in naira, with the invoice amount as secondary.

This is the whole trust proposition in one screen. A fintech product in this market gets exactly one chance: if the first payout is smaller than the app implied, the user assumes they've been quietly skimmed and they tell everyone they know. I designed the payout view to lead with the guaranteed figure and show the deductions as a readable breakdown rather than a fine-print total.

what it costEvery headline number in the product is smaller and less exciting than a competitor's. Growth teams hate this. It's the correct trade: the number that builds trust is the one that turns out to be true.
decision 02

Make escrow visible to the person it reassures

Escrow is usually designed around release: milestones, conditions, approval. That framing serves the client, who wants control over when money leaves.

But the freelancer's fear isn't about release timing. It's whether the money exists at all. So I surfaced funding as the primary state on the freelancer's side, the client has committed these funds, they are held, they are real, before any work begins. The client keeps the release control they need. The freelancer gets the reassurance they actually came for.

what it costMore friction for the client, who now has to part with money earlier and can see the freelancer watching it. Some clients will find that uncomfortable. That discomfort is the product working.
decision 03

The platform chases the money, not the freelancer

Late payment tooling normally hands the freelancer a button to send a reminder or apply a fee. Almost nobody presses it. Chasing a client for money risks the relationship, and freelancers routinely absorb a late payment rather than seem difficult.

I designed the reminders and late fees as automatic platform behaviour agreed at invoice time, not as an action the freelancer takes. The system sends it. Nobody has to be the person who sent it.

what it costLess control over a delicate relationship, and a real risk when the automation fires on a client the freelancer would have given grace to. It needed a clear override, but the default had to be automatic, because a feature people are too uncomfortable to use is not a feature.
decision 04

Trust signalled by clarity, not by padlocks

The brief asked the design to convey professionalism, trust and financial security. The reflex in African fintech is to reach for security iconography: shields, padlocks, encryption badges, bank logos.

That reads as protesting too much. I signalled trust structurally instead: every fee named, every conversion rate shown at the moment it applies, every transaction state given a plain-language explanation of what happens next and when. No security theatre anywhere in the interface.

what it costA quieter, less reassuring-looking product at first glance, and a harder pitch to stakeholders who wanted visible trust markers. Restraint is difficult to defend in a review and obvious in use.

04 — what shipped

A four-week engagement, delivered

This was a scoped design engagement ending at handoff. It didn't launch on my watch, so there are no adoption numbers and I won't invent any.

  • 13Features in the brief, reduced to one spine: get paid, and know what you're getting.
  • 2Sides of the product designed in parallel, freelancer and client, with opposite anxieties about the same transaction.
  • 4Weeks from brainstorm to handoff, including wireframes, interactive prototypes, user testing and a style guide.
  • 1Style guide covering typography, colour, iconography and component behaviour for the development team.
05 — in hindsight

What I'd do differently

Four weeks was enough to design the product and not enough to test the part that mattered most. I user-tested the invoicing and escrow flows and never put the payout breakdown in front of a freelancer receiving real money, which is the exact screen the whole trust argument rests on. I'd have cut testing time from somewhere else.

I also designed the client side second and it shows. The freelancer flows are considered; the client's funding and approval screens are competent and generic. In a two-sided product the weaker side sets the ceiling, and I gave it the leftover week.
