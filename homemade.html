01 — the context

Five surfaces, five people, one order

HomeMade is a marketplace for Nigerian-made goods, adire, crafts, local manufacturing, sold retail and wholesale across all 36 states. Five user groups touch a single order: the buyer, the seller, the dispatch rider, the platform admin, and third-party logistics partners.

I designed all five surfaces. A unified mobile app where buyers and sellers pick their role at signup, a vendor web portal for catalogue and inventory, a standalone rider app, an admin operations portal, and the marketing site. Our PM defined the scope in a signed-off specification; my job was five products that stay in agreement with each other in real time.

02 — the problem

Everyone is looking at the same order and seeing something different

A buyer fills a cart with items from three sellers and thinks she has bought one thing. The system knows it's three sub-orders, three payments, three packing jobs, three riders and three delivery timelines. She'll get three sets of updates for a purchase she made once.

Underneath that, the same order looks different again to everyone else. The seller sees a packing job. The rider sees a pickup and a drop-off. Admin sees an exception waiting to happen.

The buyer wants to know when. The seller wants to know it left. The rider wants to know what's next. Admin needs to know who to call.

One order, one truth, five presentations, and none of them can contradict another, because the moment they do, someone is on the phone to support.

03 — what I decided

Four calls across five products

decision 01

Let the buyer keep her mental model, and absorb the split

The honest system view is three separate orders. Showing that at checkout means three payment confirmations, three receipts and three tracking screens for one shopping trip, which feels like a bug.

I kept the cart and checkout singular, one summary, one payment, one confirmation, and let the split surface only where it becomes real: at delivery, where three packages genuinely arrive at different times. Order tracking is grouped under the purchase, not scattered across it.

what it costA harder refund and cancellation story, because partial states now exist inside something the buyer experiences as one order. Every one of those partial states had to be designed, and they're the least elegant screens in the product.
decision 02

Show the wholesale price you haven't earned yet

Wholesale pricing runs on minimum order quantity. Buy below it and you pay retail; the discounted tier only applies once the cart clears the threshold.

The cautious design hides the wholesale price until it's unlocked, avoiding any suggestion of a price the buyer can't have. I did the opposite, the tier and the gap to it are visible from the product page onward: this is ₦4,500 each, and ₦3,200 each at twenty units. For Tunde, who runs a boutique in Abuja and is buying to resell, that gap is the entire purchase decision, and hiding it makes the platform look expensive to exactly the buyer it wants most.

what it costA busier product page, and a retail buyer who now sees a cheaper price she isn't eligible for. That's a real irritation, and it was the right trade, the wholesale buyer is worth many times the retail one and was leaving before he got to the cart.
decision 03

Design interstate delivery as custody, not progress

A local delivery is one rider between two strangers. Interstate is worse: the seller's rider takes the package to a motor park, it travels by waybill, a partner rider in the destination state collects it, and that person delivers it. Four sets of hands, most of whom have never met.

The default pattern is a progress bar, five stages, percentage complete. That answers “how far along” when everyone is actually asking “who has my package right now.” I built it as an explicit chain of custody where each state names the person holding the item. When something stalls, admin sees a name and a number rather than a stuck bar.

what it costFive discrete confirmations is real work for a rider who is already busy, and a missed confirmation now strands an order visibly instead of quietly. That's the right failure, but it generates support tickets a vaguer design would have hidden.
decision 04

Build seller onboarding for someone who doesn't trust it

The seller we're actually recruiting is Ama: a handmade crafts business in Lagos, limited digital experience, and existing suspicion of online marketplaces. Onboarding asks her for CAC registration, documents, and an admin approval wait.

That's a long, bureaucratic ask from a platform she has no reason to believe in yet. I designed it as a resumable multi-step flow with persistent verification status, where she is, what's outstanding, and what happens next, so an incomplete application looks like progress rather than rejection. She can list products while approval is pending; they just don't go live.

what it costA more complex state model on both the seller and admin side, and a population of half-onboarded sellers with unpublished catalogues that admin has to chase. Worth it, the alternative is a form that Ama abandons at step two and never returns to.

04 — what shipped

Scope, while it's being built

The platform is in development. I'd rather show what I designed than quote numbers for something nobody has used yet.

  • 5Surfaces designed end to end: buyer and seller app, vendor portal, rider app, admin portal, marketing site.
  • 5User groups seeing the same order simultaneously, each needing a different answer from it.
  • 36States covered, with interstate routed through the motor park and waybill network that already exists.
  • AAWCAG 2.1 conformance specified across every surface, including keyboard navigation on the admin portal and scalable type on mobile.
05 — in hindsight

What I'd do differently

I treated the partner rider in the destination state as a system state rather than a person. They're the least visible participant in the chain, they have no relationship with the platform, and nothing I designed gives them a reason to care. If interstate delivery underperforms, that's the first place I'd look.

I also under-served the seller inside the unified app. One app with a role chosen at signup is right for acquisition, but running a store from a phone is a genuinely different job from browsing one, and the seller experience ended up shaped by decisions made for the buyer. The vendor web portal absorbed the work that should have been split between them.
