01 — the context

The informal economy, with receipts

Most household work in Nigeria is arranged by word of mouth. You call the plumber your neighbour used. If he doesn't show, or the price changes halfway through, or the work is bad, there's no record of what was agreed and nobody to take it to.

Anywork aggregates that market — cleaning, laundry, pest control, plumbing, carpentry, electrical, HVAC, painting and appliance repair — and puts a platform between two strangers. Two apps, two opposed sets of incentives: clients want certainty and low prices, workers want volume and fair pay for scope they can't see until they arrive.

02 — the problem

A specification that solved the business and created the design

I came in with a product requirements document that was thorough and well-reasoned. It defined a three-tier verification ladder, two entirely separate booking models, seven service categories each needing different information, two user types, and both mobile and web.

All of it justified. All of it, built literally, four products wearing one logo.

The spec was right about what the business needed. It said nothing about how any of it should feel like one thing.

That was the brief I actually worked on: absorb the complexity into a coherent product, and design a system that could carry it across both apps without either side feeling like a stripped-down version of the other.

03 — what I decided

Four calls that made it one product

decision 01

Make the bottom of the ladder look safe, not suspicious

Verification runs in three tiers. Level one is phone and OTP. Level two adds facial verification, earning a verified badge and priority in matching. Level three adds a guarantor and physical address check, earning top rated, lower fees and access to higher-paying work.

The trap is obvious once you see it: if two tiers carry badges, the third carries an absence — and an absent badge reads as a warning. That strands every new artisan on a platform they can't get a first job on, which kills supply before the product has any.

I designed level one as a stated, positive status with its own visual weight, showing what had been checked rather than leaving a gap where a badge would go. Higher tiers add signal instead of correcting a deficiency.

what it costA weaker incentive to upgrade. Making level one comfortable reduces the pressure to climb, and the tiers are how the platform funds its own trust. That tension is real and I don't think it's fully resolved.
decision 02

One booking form that changes shape, not seven forms

Each service needs different information. HVAC needs gas type, capacity and last service date. Painting needs wall condition and a colour reference. Plumbing needs issue type and affected room. Seven categories, seven field sets.

This isn't about tidiness. A worker bids on scope they can't see, and a bid made on bad information becomes a payment dispute three days later. The booking form is where disputes are prevented — so it has to extract real detail without feeling like an insurance claim.

I built it as a single progressive flow with a shared skeleton and a service-specific middle: same entry, same image upload, same summary, different questions in between. A client who books a plumber and later a painter is in the same product both times.

what it costConsiderably more specification and QA than seven standalone forms. Adding a service category later means designing into a constraint rather than from scratch.
decision 03

Quick Book and bidding as one decision, not two products

Two booking models exist for good reason. Some work is a commodity at a knowable price — book it, pay the listed amount. Some can't be priced without assessment, so the client posts a budget and workers bid.

Presented as two modes, users have to understand a distinction that only makes sense from inside the business. I designed the choice out of the interface where possible: the service determines the path, and the client meets whichever model applies as the natural next step rather than a fork they have to reason about.

what it costLess user control, and harder recovery when the automatic path is wrong for a particular job. It also made browse-before-signup harder, since the models diverge exactly where an anonymous user hits the wall.
decision 04

One design system, two apps that want opposite things

The client app is about reassurance: who is this person, what will it cost, what happens if it goes wrong. The worker app is about throughput: how many requests, how much, how far, accept or decline.

I built a single system across mobile and web serving both, with shared foundations and role-specific density — the worker's job list tighter and faster, the client's provider cards slower and more evidential. Same components, different tuning.

what it costEvery component effectively designed twice, at two densities, with constant temptation to fork into two systems. Holding one is more work up front and much less by the fourth screen.

04 — what shipped

Scope, not adoption numbers

This was a complete design engagement, start to finish. I'd rather show what exists than quote adoption figures for a product I don't measure.

05 — in hindsight

What I'd do differently

I designed the verification ladder before speaking to artisans about what they'd actually be willing to hand over. Facial verification and a guarantor are significant asks in a market where people are cautious about giving a platform their details, and I specified the incentives for climbing before understanding the reasons not to.

I also treated the dispute path as a support workflow rather than a design surface. It's the moment the whole trust promise is tested, and it's the least designed part of the product.
