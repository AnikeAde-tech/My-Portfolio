01 — the context

Everyone's photos, nobody's album

Every event produces hundreds of photographs and scatters them across two hundred camera rolls. The good ones — the shot of you dancing that you never saw, the one where your grandmother is laughing — sit on a phone belonging to someone whose surname you don't know.

NextVibe collects them. Guests scan a QR code and upload into one shared album. Organisers get the content and the engagement data. Brands sponsor moments instead of banners. It runs across weddings, birthdays, festivals and corporate events, and it serves three groups who want very different things from the same screens.

02 — the problem

Every user is a first-time user, forever

The founding constraint was no app download. Guests scan a code and they're in.

That decision is right — nobody installs software at a wedding — and it removes every tool a designer normally leans on. No onboarding sequence, because there's no second session to spread it over. No account, so no persistent identity, so no obvious way to answer which of these six hundred photos are mine. No push notifications to bring anyone back. No tutorial, because everyone arrives mid-party and leaves ninety seconds later.

I was designing for someone standing up, in bad light, holding a drink, who will never see this screen again.

Everything had to work on first contact or not at all.

03 — what I decided

Four calls that made ninety seconds enough

decision 01

The QR landing is the entire product, not the entrance to it

Conventional structure puts a landing page in front and the real interface behind it. With no download and no account, that costs you the only ninety seconds you get.

I collapsed the whole first-time experience into the screen the QR code opens: what this is, whose event, and the upload action, immediately available. No splash, no explanation, no “get started.” Someone who scans and uploads without reading anything is the success case, not the edge case.

what it costAlmost no room to explain the rest of the product — the games, the tags, the postcards — so discovery of everything beyond upload is weak. Attendees who'd have loved the full thing mostly never find out it exists.
decision 02

The game is the capture mechanic, not a feature beside it

Left alone, guests photograph their own table and nothing else. The album ends up as four hundred selfies and no record of the event.

Photo challenges, trivia and scavenger hunts exist as engagement features, but I designed them as the thing that actually populates the album — prompts that send people to photograph the cake, the band, the person they haven't spoken to yet. The leaderboard gives them a reason to keep going.

what it costGamification is wrong for some events. A leaderboard at a wedding can read as tacky, so the mechanic had to be optional and tonally adjustable by event type — which meant designing it twice, loud and quiet.
decision 03

Tag from what the system already knows

VibeTags make photos searchable, which requires structure — and structure normally requires someone to label things. Nobody at a party is going to fill in metadata.

I designed tagging to come from context the platform already holds: which event, which stage, which set time, which segment of the evening. The guest uploads; the tags attach themselves. At a festival that means photos organised by artist without a single person choosing a category.

what it costThe tags are only as good as the organiser's setup, so the work moved rather than disappearing. A badly configured event produces a badly organised album, and the person who suffers is the guest, not the organiser who caused it.
decision 04

Sponsorship attaches to a moment, or it doesn't ship

Brands pay for events, and the usual mechanism is an interstitial or a watermark — which is exactly the thing that makes a memory feel like an advert.

I kept sponsorship to surfaces where a brand can add rather than interrupt: a challenge someone chooses to play, a postcard frame, a stage tag. Nothing sits between a guest and their photos.

what it costFar harder to sell than guaranteed impressions, and it gives brands less control than they're used to asking for. It's the constraint most likely to be argued away commercially, and it's the one I'd fight hardest for.

04 — what shipped

Live, across four event types

The product is live and running real events. The numbers I have are organiser-side and not mine to publish, so here's the scope instead.

  • 3Distinct audiences designed for in one product: attendees, organisers and sponsoring brands.
  • 0App downloads required. The entire guest experience runs in a mobile browser.
  • 4+Event types the same product has to fit: weddings, birthdays, festivals and corporate events, each with its own tone.
  • 1Ninety-second window, which was the real design budget for every guest-facing screen.
05 — in hindsight

What I'd do differently

I optimised so hard for the first ninety seconds that I under-designed the return. The best moment in this product is opening the full album a few days later, when the event is over and someone finds the photo they didn't know existed — and that experience got a fraction of the attention the upload flow did.

I also should have pushed harder on the identity problem instead of designing around it. “Which of these are mine” is the question every guest actually has, and no-download made it inconvenient enough that we let it stay unsolved longer than we should have.
