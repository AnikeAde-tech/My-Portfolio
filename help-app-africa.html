01 — the context

A live product that couldn't stop to be fixed

Help App Africa connects people with local service providers. By the time I came in on a five-month contract, the product had been shipping for long enough to accumulate the usual debt: features layered on features, three different ways to do the same thing, and a team that knew it was bad but had no map of how bad.

The brief was “redesign the core platform.” The constraint was that the release train couldn't stop and the contract was five months, not eighteen.

02 — the problem

Everything was broken, which is the same as nothing being prioritised

I started with a UX audit — every screen, every state, scored against task completion rather than aesthetics. It produced a list of about forty problems, which is a useless artefact on its own. A list of forty problems tells a team to panic.

So I overlaid traffic. Where were people actually going, and where were they failing? Three journeys accounted for the overwhelming majority of sessions and nearly all of the failures. The other thirty-odd problems were real and almost entirely irrelevant.

“An audit that doesn't rank its own findings has just moved the hard decision onto someone else's desk.”
03 — what I decided

Two calls that made five months enough

decision 01

Fix three journeys. Leave the rest ugly.

The instinct in the room was a full redesign — consistent, complete, satisfying. It would have shipped in month nine of a five-month contract.

I argued for depth on the three high-traffic journeys and an explicit, written decision to leave everything else alone. Not “we'll get to it” — a stated boundary, so nobody had to relitigate it every sprint.

what it costA visibly inconsistent product for two quarters, and a recurring conversation with stakeholders about why other screens still looked old. I'd defend it: the alternative was a beautiful redesign that arrived after I'd left.
decision 02

Ship behind the existing visual language, then restyle

Changing structure and styling at once makes it impossible to tell which change moved the metric — and doubles the engineering review.

We shipped the new flows in the old skin first, measured, then applied the new styling as a separate release.

what it costTwo release cycles instead of one, and an interim state that looked like nothing had changed. But we knew which half of the work was doing the lifting.
04 — what happened

The numbers, and the caveats

  • +28%Task completion across the three redesigned journeys.
  • +35%CSAT, measured by the existing in-app survey — small sample, directionally clear.
  • +30%Feature adoption, concentrated in the two features we moved out of a buried menu.
05 — in hindsight

What I'd do differently

I should have written the “not doing this” list as a shared artefact in week one instead of week five. Until it existed, every stand-up re-opened the same scope argument, and I spent goodwill defending a decision that was already made.

I also under-invested in handover. The contract ended with three journeys rebuilt and no documented rationale for the team inheriting it — which means some of those decisions have probably been quietly undone by now.
